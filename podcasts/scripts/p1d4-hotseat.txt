ACE: Penny, you're up first. A taxpayer exercised incentive stock options in 2025, with a spread of $50,000, and she still held the shares on December 31. What's the effect for 2025? A, $50,000 of wages on her W-2. B, nothing at all for regular tax or the AMT. C, no regular tax income, but a $50,000 AMT adjustment. D, a $50,000 long-term capital gain.
PENNY: My coworker at Gadget Works did this! She cried in the break room. I think it's C. No regular tax, but the AMT gets you.
[correct]
HOOT: Correct. The break room tears were justified.
ACE: Exercising an incentive stock option isn't a regular tax event. But for the AMT, the spread, which is fair market value minus the exercise price, is added to alternative minimum taxable income in the year of exercise if the stock is still held at year end. And the stock gets a higher AMT basis.
HOOT: If she had sold the shares in the same year she exercised, that is a disqualifying disposition. The spread is ordinary income for regular tax, and there is no AMT adjustment. And to get long-term treatment for regular tax, she must hold the shares at least two years from grant and one year from exercise.
ACE: Here's the good news. AMT caused by timing items, like the ISO spread or depreciation differences, creates a minimum tax credit on Form 8801. It can reduce regular tax in later years, and it carries forward indefinitely. AMT caused by exclusion items, like state and local taxes or the standard deduction, never comes back.
ACE: Memory trick: "Two tax systems. You pay the higher one." And "exercise and hold, the AMT gets told."

ACE: Rosa. A sole proprietor has net profit of $40,000 in 2025 and no wages. About how much is his self-employment tax? A, $6,120. B, $3,060. C, $5,652. D, $0, because he takes the standard deduction.

[buzzer]
HOOT: Wrong. So close. Like a cake that's raw in the middle.
ROSA: Don't you talk about my cakes.
ACE: It's C. You don't apply the 15.3 percent to the whole net profit. First, net earnings from self-employment are 92.35 percent of net profit. $40,000 times 92.35 percent is $36,940. Then times 15.3 percent is about $5,652. And half of that, about $2,826, is deductible as an adjustment to income.
HOOT: The 15.3 percent is 12.4 percent Social Security, up to the 2025 wage base of $176,100, plus 2.9 percent Medicare on all of it. Wages from a job count first toward that $176,100. And self-employment tax applies at $400 of net earnings, even if no income tax is owed at all.
ACE: Trap alert: the QBI deduction, the standard deduction and itemized deductions don't reduce self-employment tax. Only the business expenses do.
ACE: Memory trick: "Ninety-two thirty-five, then fifteen three." First you shrink it, then you tax it. "Be your own boss, pay both halves."

HOOT: Lulu. A family pays their sixteen-year-old babysitter, who is a high school student, $3,000 in 2025. Do they owe Social Security and Medicare tax on her wages? LULU: That's Tess! Tess is my babysitter! She's sixteen and she's a student! And she lets me stay up until eight thirty. So B.
[correct]

ACE: For 2025, a household employer owes Social Security and Medicare if they pay one household employee cash wages of $2,800 or more. But wages paid to anyone under eighteen at any time in the year are exempt, unless household work is their principal occupation. Being a student means it isn't. Wages paid to your spouse, your child under twenty-one, or usually your parent are also exempt.
HOOT: A household employee is someone where the family controls not only what work is done, but how. A nanny, a housekeeper, a private caretaker. For 2025, $2,800 or more of cash wages to one household employee triggers Social Security and Medicare. $1,000 or more in total cash wages in any calendar quarter, this year or last, triggers federal unemployment tax. Income tax withholding is only by agreement.
ACE: And it's all reported on Schedule H with the family's Form 1040. They need an EIN, and they give the employee a W-2 by January 31.
ACE: Memory trick: "Twenty-eight hundred for the nanny, a thousand a quarter for FUTA, and teens who are students are free."

ACE: Penny. A married couple files jointly. She earns $210,000 in wages, and her employer withheld the 0.9 percent Additional Medicare Tax on the $10,000 above $200,000. Her husband has no income. What happens? A, they owe the 0.9 percent on $10,000. B, they owe nothing, and the withholding is credited back on Form 8959. C, they owe the 0.9 percent on all $210,000. [correct]
HOOT: Correct. Penny Pocket understands employers. Disturbing.
ACE: The Additional Medicare Tax is 0.9 percent of wages and self-employment income above $250,000 for married filing jointly, $125,000 for married filing separately, and $200,000 for everyone else. Those thresholds aren't indexed for inflation. There's no employer match. An employer must withhold it on wages it pays over $200,000, regardless of filing status, and it's reconciled on Form 8959.
HOOT: And its cousin, the net investment income tax. 3.8 percent of the lesser of net investment income, or modified AGI over the same thresholds: $250,000 joint, $125,000 separate, $200,000 others. Form 8960. It covers interest, dividends, capital gains, rents, royalties and passive business income. It does not cover wages, self-employment income, IRA and retirement plan distributions, tax-exempt interest, or Social Security.
ACE: Memory trick: "Point nine on pay, three point eight on investments, same thresholds." Two fifty, one twenty-five, two hundred. And they never grow up.

ACE: Rosa, redemption round. A beneficiary receives a $2,000 final paycheck that her grandfather earned before he died, paid by his employer after his death. Who reports it as income? A, it goes on the grandfather's final Form 1040. B, the beneficiary, as income in respect of a decedent. C, nobody, it's an inheritance. D, the employer.

ACE: It's B. Income in respect of a decedent, or IRD, is income the person had a right to but didn't receive before death. Final wages, traditional IRA and retirement plan balances, accrued interest, installment sale payments still due. It's taxed to whoever receives it, in the year they receive it, with the same character it would have had for the decedent. It's not on the final return.
HOOT: And IRD gets no stepped-up basis. Inherited stock gets a new basis at death. An inherited traditional IRA does not. Every dollar out is taxable to the beneficiary. If the estate paid federal estate tax on the IRD, the recipient gets an itemized deduction for that estate tax.
ACE: And the bakery reports the same-year wages on Walt's W-2 for Social Security and Medicare only, with no income tax withholding, and on a Form 1099-MISC to Penny.
ACE: Memory trick: "IRD: taxed to whoever gets it, never stepped up."

ACE: Ministers have dual status. For income tax, they're usually employees of the church. The housing allowance is excluded from income tax up to the least of three numbers: the amount designated, actual housing costs, or the fair rental value furnished plus utilities. Here that's $20,000. But the whole allowance is included in net earnings for self-employment tax.
HOOT: Exam tip: a minister who is conscientiously opposed, on religious principles, to public insurance can apply for an exemption from SE tax with Form 4361. ACE: It's B. The failure-to-file penalty is five percent of the unpaid tax for each month or part of a month, up to twenty-five percent. The failure-to-pay penalty is half a percent a month, up to twenty-five percent. But in any month both apply, the failure-to-file penalty is reduced by the failure-to-pay penalty. So the combined rate is five percent a month. Five percent of $2,000 is $100.

ACE: Memory trick: "Late filing hurts ten times more than late paying." Five versus half. And together, they're still five.

HOOT: Rosa. A taxpayer's 2024 AGI was $200,000, and her 2024 tax was $40,000. Her 2025 tax is $60,000. What's the smallest total of 2025 withholding and timely estimated payments that avoids the estimated tax penalty? A, $40,000. B, $54,000. C, $44,000. D, $60,000.

[buzzer]
HOOT: Wrong. The electric company is right to be confused.
ACE: It's C. Normally you avoid the estimated tax penalty by paying the smaller of ninety percent of this year's tax or one hundred percent of last year's tax. But if last year's AGI was over $150,000, or $75,000 married filing separately, the prior-year test is 110 percent. 110 percent of $40,000 is $44,000. Ninety percent of this year's $60,000 is $54,000. The smaller is $44,000.
HOOT: Also, no penalty if you owe less than $1,000 after withholding and credits. And withholding is treated as paid evenly through the year, even if it came in December.
ACE: Memory trick: "Ninety this year, a hundred last year, a hundred ten if you're fancy." Fancy means AGI over one-fifty.

ACE: False. Form 4868 extends the time to file to October 15, not the time to pay. Interest and the failure-to-pay penalty run from April 15 on any unpaid tax. But if you've paid at least ninety percent by April 15 and pay the rest with a return filed within the extension, there's no failure-to-pay penalty, just interest.
HOOT: And relief. Reasonable cause, such as serious illness or a disaster, when the taxpayer used ordinary care and prudence. Or first-time abatement: no significant penalties in the prior three years, all required returns filed, and the tax paid or arranged. First-time abatement covers failure to file, failure to pay and failure to deposit. It does not cover the accuracy-related penalty or the estimated tax penalty.
ACE: And the accuracy-related penalty is twenty percent of an underpayment from negligence, or from a substantial understatement, which is more than the larger of ten percent of the correct tax or $5,000. Fraud is seventy-five percent.
ACE: Memory trick: "An extension buys time to file, never to pay."
