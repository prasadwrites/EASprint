ACE: A small business taxpayer, with average annual gross receipts of $31 million or less for 2025 and not a tax shelter, can use the cash method, can skip formal inventories by treating them as non-incidental materials and supplies or following its books, and is exempt from UNICAP. Others value inventory at cost or the lower of cost or market, using FIFO, LIFO with Form 970, or specific identification.
ACE: The cash method counts income when actually or constructively received, and expenses when paid, with a twelve-month rule for prepaid expenses. ACE: Section 162 allows a deduction for ordinary and necessary business expenses. Ordinary means common in the trade. Necessary means helpful and appropriate. Personal and capital costs aren't deductible. Compensation must be reasonable and for services performed. Business real estate taxes, payroll taxes and personal property taxes are deductible. ACE: Start-up costs, and separately, organizational costs. You can deduct up to $5,000 in the year the business begins, reduced by start-up costs over $50,000, so it's zero at $55,000. The rest is amortized over 180 months from the month the business starts. And if the business never begins, nothing is deductible as start-up costs.
ACE: Business bad debts are an ordinary deduction when they're wholly or partly worthless, using the specific charge-off method, but only if the amount was previously included in income or actually loaned. A cash-method business can't deduct unpaid receivables. Nonbusiness bad debts are short-term capital losses, only when totally worthless.

Simplified method: five dollars per square foot, up to 300 square feet, so $1,500 maximum. Regular method: actual costs on Form 8829. Limited to business income. And NO deduction for employees.

ACE: Businesses can elect each year to expense items of $2,500 or less per invoice or item, or $5,000 with an applicable financial statement. Repairs and maintenance are deductible. Improvements, which are betterments, restorations or adaptations, like a new roof, are capitalized and depreciated.

ACE: Travel. You're away from your tax home overnight, long enough to need sleep or rest, for business. Transportation and lodging are one hundred percent deductible, and meals are fifty percent. On a domestic trip that's primarily business, the full round-trip fare is deductible, but personal side trips aren't. A temporary assignment must be one year or less. And a companion's costs aren't deductible unless the companion is a bona fide employee.
ROSA: Gerald isn't an employee.
HOOT: Gerald is a jar.

HOOT: Meals. Fifty percent if not lavish, with the taxpayer or an employee present, with a business contact. Entertainment: ZERO percent, though food at an entertainment event is fifty percent if it's separately stated. One hundred percent for employee recreational and social events, meals sold to customers, and meals reported as compensation. Meals for the convenience of the employer: fifty percent through 2025, zero after.
ACE: Cars. The 2025 standard mileage rate is seventy cents a business mile, plus parking and tolls. You must choose it in the car's first business year, and you can't use it for five or more cars at once or after claiming MACRS, section 179 or bonus. The actual method is the business-use percentage of costs, plus depreciation, and you need more than fifty percent business use for section 179 or bonus. Commuting is never deductible. Keep a mileage log.

ACE: MACRS, the general depreciation system. Five-year property, like cars and computers. Seven-year property, like furniture, most equipment, and property with no class life. Fifteen-year for land improvements. 27.5-year for residential rental, 39-year for nonresidential real property. Land is never depreciated. Personal property uses 200 percent declining balance and a half-year convention, or mid-quarter if more than forty percent was placed in service in the fourth quarter. Real property uses straight line with mid-month.
HOOT: Section 179 for 2025. Elect up to $2,500,000, phased out dollar for dollar over $4,000,000 of purchases. Limited to business taxable income, with the excess carried forward. More than fifty percent business use. Heavy SUVs are capped at $31,300. It covers equipment, off-the-shelf software, and certain nonresidential improvements: roofs, HVAC and security systems. Form 4562.
ACE: Bonus depreciation for 2025: one hundred percent for qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025, and that's permanent now. Forty percent if acquired before January 20, 2025 and placed in service in 2025. New or used property with a recovery period of twenty years or less. It's automatic unless you elect out by class, and there's no income limit, so it can create an NOL. The order is section 179, then bonus, then regular MACRS.
ROSA: Bertha was bought in March. After January 19.
ACE: So she qualifies for one hundred percent bonus. 179 first, bonus next, MACRS last.
HOOT: Listed property, like cars and other transportation property, needs more than fifty percent qualified business use for section 179, bonus, and accelerated MACRS. Otherwise, ADS straight line. If business use later drops to fifty percent or less, the excess over straight line is recaptured as ordinary income. Passenger cars also face the luxury auto caps.
ACE: And depreciation, including 179 and bonus, reduces basis. For section 1245 property, gain up to the depreciation taken is ordinary income, and any remainder is section 1231. For real property depreciated straight line, unrecaptured section 1250 gain is taxed at a maximum of twenty-five percent for individuals. Form 4797.

ACE: Adjusted basis is cost, including sales tax, freight and installation, plus improvements, minus depreciation allowed or allowable, including 179 and bonus, minus casualty losses. Gain is amount realized, meaning cash, plus fair market value of property received, plus liabilities assumed by the buyer, minus selling costs, minus adjusted basis.
HOOT: Section 1231 property is depreciable business property and land held more than one year. Section 1245 recapture is ordinary first. A net section 1231 gain is long-term capital gain, except it's ordinary to the extent of unrecaptured net section 1231 losses from the prior five years. A net section 1231 loss is ordinary. Held one year or less: ordinary. Form 4797.
MILO: And the trade-in is a 1031 exchange!
ACE: No. Section 1031 is for real property only, held for business or investment. Not inventory, and U.S. real property isn't like-kind to foreign real property. Wheels aren't like-kind. Only land and buildings are.
ACE: For a real 1031, in a deferred exchange you identify the replacement in forty-five days and receive it within 180 days, or by the return due date if earlier. Gain recognized is the lesser of realized gain or boot, which includes cash, other property and net liability relief. Basis carries over. Form 8824.
HOOT: Related parties. Section 267 disallows a loss on a sale between related persons: a spouse, siblings, ancestors, descendants, and entities more than fifty percent owned, directly or indirectly. NOT aunts, uncles, cousins or in-laws. The buyer may use the disallowed loss to offset a later gain. Section 1239 makes gain on depreciable property sold to a related person ordinary. And in a related-party 1031, there's a two-year holding requirement.
ROSA: So if I sold some of my stock to Penny at a loss?
ACE: Penny isn't your sibling, your ancestor or your descendant. She's Walt's granddaughter, so she's not on the list, and the loss can count. Sell it to your sister at a loss, and it's disallowed.

HOOT: Installment sales. At least one payment after the year of sale. Gain recognized equals payment times gross profit percentage, which is gross profit divided by contract price. Depreciation recapture is all taxed in the year of sale. Not for inventory, dealer sales, or losses. You can elect out. Form 6252.

HOOT: FICA for 2025. Six point two percent Social Security, on a wage base of $176,100, plus one point four five percent Medicare, from each side. Seven point six five percent each. The Additional Medicare Tax of nine-tenths of a percent is withheld over $200,000, employee only. Form 941 quarterly, due April 30, July 31, October 31 and January 31. Deposits: monthly if the lookback period total is $50,000 or less, otherwise semiweekly. And the $100,000 next-day rule. Form 944 for very small employers.
ACE: FUTA. Employer only. Six percent of the first $7,000 per employee, with a state credit up to five point four percent, so usually zero point six percent, or $42 per employee. A smaller credit in credit reduction states. It applies if you paid $1,500 or more in wages in a quarter, or had an employee in twenty or more weeks. Form 940 annually by January 31, or February 10 if fully deposited. Deposit when the liability is more than $500.
HOOT: W-2s to employees and the SSA by January 31. 1099-NEC to contractors, generally not corporations, but attorneys always, by January 31. The threshold is $600 for 2025 payments, and $2,000 for payments after December 31, 2025, indexed after 2026. Get Form W-9. No correct TIN means twenty-four percent backup withholding.

ACE: Owner-level loss limits apply in order. One: basis. Three: passive activity, section 469, Form 8582. Four: the excess business loss limit, section 461(l), Form 461. Disallowed amounts carry forward under each rule.

HOOT: The excess business loss. For noncorporate taxpayers, a net business loss over $313,000 single, or $626,000 married filing jointly, for 2025, is disallowed and becomes an NOL carryforward. It's applied after the basis, at-risk and passive limits. Made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Not for C corporations. Form 461.
ACE: A net operating loss is business deductions over income; individuals exclude nonbusiness deductions. NOLs from after 2020 have no carryback, except farming, two years back, and they carry forward indefinitely. NOLs from after 2017 are limited to eighty percent of taxable income figured without the NOL. Pre-2018 NOLs have no eighty percent limit. Form 172.
HOOT: QBI at the business level. Up to twenty percent of qualified business income from pass-throughs, not C corporations, limited to twenty percent of taxable income minus net capital gain. Excludes wages, guaranteed payments, S corporation reasonable compensation, and investment income. 2025 threshold: $197,300, or $394,600 joint, with a phase-in of $50,000 or $100,000, where the W-2 wage and UBIA limit applies and specified service businesses phase out.
ACE: And the general business credit, Form 3800, combines credits like the research credit, the work opportunity credit, the pension plan startup credit, the small employer health insurance credit and the disabled access credit. It's limited to net income tax minus the greater of tentative minimum tax or twenty-five percent of net regular tax over $25,000. Unused credits carry back one year and forward twenty.

HOOT: Permanent differences never reverse: fines and penalties, fifty percent of meals, key-person life insurance premiums, tax-exempt interest, and federal income tax expense for a C corporation. Temporary differences reverse: tax versus book depreciation, including 179 and bonus, a bad debt allowance versus direct write-off, and prepaid income.
ACE: Schedule M-1 reconciles book income to taxable income. M-2 rolls forward equity: retained earnings on the 1120, partners' capital on the 1065, and the AAA on the 1120-S. M-3 replaces M-1 for corporations with total assets of $10 million or more. For partnerships, assets of $10 million or more, receipts of $35 million or more, or a large reportable partner. It separates permanent and temporary differences.
HOOT: Schedule L is the balance sheet per books, beginning and end of year, on the 1120, 1120-S and 1065, and its equity ties to M-2.
