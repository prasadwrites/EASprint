ACE: Penny, warm-up question. A bakery's beginning inventory is $4,000. It buys $60,000 of flour and supplies for resale during the year, and ending inventory is $3,000. What's the cost of goods sold? A, $67,000. B, $61,000. C, $60,000. D, $57,000.
PENNY: Start plus bought minus left. Four plus sixty minus three. Sixty-one. B.
[correct]
ACE: Cost of goods sold is beginning inventory, plus purchases, plus labor, materials and other costs, minus ending inventory. Gross profit is net receipts minus cost of goods sold. And this year's ending inventory becomes next year's beginning inventory.
HOOT: A small business taxpayer, average annual gross receipts of $31 million or less for 2025 and not a tax shelter, can use the cash method, skip formal inventories, and is exempt from the uniform capitalization rules. Changing an accounting method generally needs Form 3115.

HOOT: Rosa. A customer pays the bakery $6,000 in cash for a wedding cake deposit, and then $5,000 in cash the next day for the balance. What must the bakery do? A, nothing, each payment is under $10,000. B, file Form 8300 within fifteen days. C, file a 1099-K. D, refuse the cash.

The business also gives the payer written notice by January 31 of the next year, and keeps a copy for five years.

ACE: Milo. Start-up costs. In 2025, a man opens a dog-walking business after spending $52,000 of start-up costs. How much can he deduct immediately, before amortization? A, $5,000. B, $3,000. C, $52,000. D, $0.
MILO: Milo's Mutts! The relaunch! Okay. The rule is five thousand. A!
[buzzer]
HOOT: Wrong. You forgot the shrink.
ACE: It's B. A business can deduct up to $5,000 of start-up costs in the year it begins, but that $5,000 is reduced dollar for dollar by start-up costs over $50,000. $52,000 is $2,000 over, so the immediate deduction is $3,000. The rest is amortized over 180 months, starting with the month the business begins. Organizational costs for a corporation or partnership have their own separate, identical $5,000 and $50,000 rule. And if the business never begins, none of it is deductible as start-up costs.
ACE: Memory trick: "Five grand now, shrinking past fifty, the rest over fifteen years." 180 months is fifteen years.

ACE: Penny. Repairs versus improvements. A bakery replaces its entire roof for $30,000. How is it treated? A, deducted as a repair. B, capitalized and depreciated, because it restores the property. C, deducted under the de minimis safe harbor. D, half and half.
PENNY: The whole roof? That's not fixing a leak. B.
[correct]
ACE: Repairs and maintenance that keep property in ordinary operating condition are deductible. Improvements are capitalized and depreciated: anything that's a betterment, a restoration, or an adaptation to a new use. Replacing a major component, like the whole roof, restores the property. The de minimis safe harbor lets a business expense items costing $2,500 or less per invoice or item, or $5,000 with an applicable financial statement, if it makes the annual election and expenses them on its books.
HOOT: And for section 162, an expense must be ordinary, common in the trade, and necessary, helpful and appropriate. Fines paid to a government are never deductible.
ACE: Memory trick: "Better, restore, adapt: capitalize. Keep it running: deduct." B, R, A: Big Repairs Add value.

[correct]
DEE: I got a depreciation question right! On a rig! Lulu!
LULU: Good job, Mom!
ACE: Under MACRS, most equipment and furniture is seven-year property, using the 200 percent declining balance method with the half-year convention. The first-year rate is 14.29 percent, so $10,000 times 14.29 percent is $1,429. Cars and computers are five-year. Land improvements are fifteen-year. Residential rental buildings are 27.5 years and nonresidential buildings 39, straight line, mid-month. And land is never depreciated.

ACE: Penny. Section 179. A business buys $80,000 of equipment in 2025 and wants to expense all of it under section 179. Its business taxable income before section 179 is $60,000. How much section 179 can it deduct for 2025? A, $80,000. B, $60,000. C, $2,500,000. D, $20,000.
PENNY: Section 179 can't make a loss. So it's capped at sixty thousand. The rest carries forward. B.
[correct]
ACE: For 2025, a business can elect to expense up to $2,500,000 under section 179, reduced dollar for dollar once purchases go over $4,000,000. But the deduction is limited to business taxable income, and the excess carries forward. The property must be used more than fifty percent for business.
HOOT: Bonus depreciation is different. For 2025, it is one hundred percent for qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025. Property acquired before January 20, 2025, and placed in service in 2025 gets forty percent. And bonus has no income limit. It can create a loss.
ACE: Memory trick: "179 first, bonus next, MACRS last." And "179 can't make you lose. Bonus can."

HOOT: Rosa. Business assets. The bakery trades in an old delivery van with an adjusted basis of $10,000 for a new van, getting an $18,000 trade-in allowance. The old van had $15,000 of depreciation taken. How is the result treated? A, a like-kind exchange, no gain. B, $8,000 of ordinary income. C, $8,000 of capital gain. D, $18,000 of income.
ROSA: Oh, I know vans aren't like each other for taxes anymore. So it's a sale. Eighteen minus ten is eight. And it's a vehicle, so... capital gain. C.
[buzzer]
HOOT: Half right. Which means wrong.
ACE: It's B. You're right that vehicles don't qualify for like-kind treatment after 2017, so the trade-in is a sale. The gain is $8,000. But on section 1245 property, like a van or equipment, gain up to the depreciation taken is recaptured as ordinary income. The van had $15,000 of depreciation, more than the $8,000 gain, so all of it is ordinary.
HOOT: The rest of the story. Section 1231 property is business depreciable property and land held more than one year. Recapture comes out first as ordinary. A net section 1231 gain is treated as long-term capital gain, except it's ordinary to the extent of net section 1231 losses in the prior five years. A net section 1231 loss is ordinary. It's all on Form 4797.
ACE: Memory trick: "Recapture first, ordinary always. Then 1231 gets the leftovers."

ACE: Under section 267, a loss on a sale between related persons is disallowed. Related persons include a spouse, siblings, ancestors and descendants, and an entity the person owns more than fifty percent of, directly or indirectly. Aunts, uncles, cousins and in-laws are not related for this rule. If the buyer later sells the property at a gain, it can use the disallowed loss to reduce that gain.

ACE: Memory trick: "Family is up, down and sideways, but not diagonal." Parents, kids, siblings: related. Aunts, uncles, cousins: not.

ACE: The common-law test is the right to control what work is done and how it's done. Evidence comes in three categories: behavioral, like instructions and training; financial, like investment, profit or loss, and how they're paid; and the relationship, like contracts, benefits and permanence. Labels don't decide it. Form SS-8 asks the IRS for a determination.
HOOT: And the employer's taxes for 2025. FICA is 6.2 percent Social Security up to $176,100 plus 1.45 percent Medicare, from each side. Federal unemployment tax is employer only: six percent of the first $7,000 per employee, but with the full state credit of 5.4 percent, it is 0.6 percent, or $42 per employee.
ACE: And Form W-2 and Form 1099-NEC are due January 31. For 2025 payments, the 1099-NEC threshold is $600. For payments after December 31, 2025, it's $2,000.
ACE: Memory trick: "Control the how, and they're your employee." And "FUTA is forty-two bucks a head."

ACE: Penny. Losses. A single owner has a 2025 business loss of $400,000, after the basis, at-risk and passive rules. How is it treated? A, deduct all $400,000. B, deduct $313,000 and carry $87,000 forward as a net operating loss. C, deduct $3,000. D, carry all of it back two years.
PENNY: The excess business loss rule. For 2025, single is three hundred thirteen thousand. The rest is an NOL. B.
[correct]
ACE: Owner-level loss limits apply in this order: basis, then at-risk on Form 6198, then passive activity on Form 8582, then the excess business loss limit on Form 461. For 2025, a noncorporate taxpayer's net business loss over $313,000 single, or $626,000 joint, is disallowed and becomes a net operating loss carryforward.
HOOT: And NOLs. Losses arising after 2020 cannot be carried back, except farming losses, which go back two years. They carry forward indefinitely. And for losses after 2017, the NOL deduction is limited to eighty percent of taxable income figured without it.
ACE: Memory trick: "Basis, at-risk, passive, then excess." Like doors in a hallway, in that order. And for NOLs: "No going back, forward forever, eighty percent at a time."

HOOT: Rosa. Reading the books. A C corporation's book income is $100,000. Its books include a $2,000 government fine, $15,000 of federal income tax expense, and $4,000 of tax-exempt interest. What is its taxable income? A, $100,000. B, $113,000. C, $121,000. D, $96,000.

ACE: Schedule M-1 reconciles book income to taxable income. Permanent differences never reverse: fines and penalties, fifty percent of meals, tax-exempt interest, federal income tax expense. Temporary differences reverse over time: tax versus book depreciation, bad debt allowances. Corporations with total assets of $10 million or more file Schedule M-3 instead of M-1. And when total receipts and total assets are both under $250,000, a corporation can skip Schedules L, M-1 and M-2.
ACE: Memory trick: "Books say profit. M-1 says taxable."
