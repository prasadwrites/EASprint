ACE: Rosa, here's your first question. A taxpayer was born on January 1, 1961. For tax year 2025, is she considered age sixty-five or older for the additional standard deduction? A, no, because she turns sixty-five in 2026. B, yes, because you reach an age on the day before your birthday. C, only if she's also blind. D, only if she files jointly.

[correct]

ACE: Here's the deeper part. For tax purposes, you reach an age on the day before your birthday. Rosa turned sixty-five on December 31, 2025, so she's sixty-five for all of 2025. For 2025, an unmarried taxpayer who's sixty-five or older adds $2,000 to the standard deduction. A married spouse who's sixty-five or older adds $1,600 each.
HOOT: So Rosa's 2025 standard deduction, filing single, is $15,750 plus $2,000. That is $17,750.
ACE: Memory trick: New Year's babies age on New Year's Eve. If the birthday is January 1, they're a year older for the year that just ended.

HOOT: A married couple marries on December 31 at 11:59 PM. Which filing statuses can they use for that year? A, single only. B, married filing jointly or married filing separately. C, head of household. D, single for the first eleven months and married for December.
MILO: Penny and I got married at 11:59 PM on New Year's Eve! And I remember Ace saying it counts as married for the whole year. B!
[correct]
ACE: Right. Marital status is decided on the last day of the year. Married on December 31, even at one minute to midnight, means married for the whole year. The flip side is a final decree of divorce or separate maintenance by December 31, which means unmarried for the whole year. And if a spouse died during the year, the survivor is still treated as married for that year and can file jointly.
HOOT: Answer D is my favorite wrong answer. There is no such thing as being married for one month. Marriage is a December 31 snapshot.
ACE: Memory trick: the tax year asks one question on New Year's Eve. "Who are you holding hands with right now?" That's your status for the whole year.

ACE: Penny, your turn. Penny, this one's a classic. Under the substantial presence test, which days count for 2025? A, all days in 2025, 2024 and 2023 added together. B, all days in 2025, one-third of the days in 2024, and one-sixth of the days in 2023, with at least thirty-one days in 2025. C, only days in 2025, with at least 183. D, one-half of every year.
PENNY: Ooh. I made flashcards for this. B. All of this year, a third of last year, a sixth of the year before, and you need at least thirty-one days this year, and the total has to be at least 183.
[correct]
HOOT: Correct, and with the thirty-one-day part, which most people forget. Two Hoot points.
PENNY: Why two?
HOOT: The flashcards were laminated. I respect that.
ACE: Let's do the math the exam will make you do. Sven was in the U.S. 120 days in 2025, 150 in 2024 and 180 in 2023. 120, plus one-third of 150, which is 50, plus one-sixth of 180, which is 30. That's 200. He also has more than thirty-one days this year. So he's a resident alien for 2025.
HOOT: And here is the trap. Days as an exempt individual, such as many students on F or J visas, do not count. And a person with a green card at any time during the year is a resident alien under the green card test, even if they spent only three days here. Do not run the day count when the green card already answered the question.
ACE: Memory trick: think "one, a third, a sixth". The years fade like a photo. This year is full color. Last year is a third. The year before is barely a ghost.
MILO: Speaking of ghosts, Hoot, your eyeshade is on backwards.

ACE: Right. An ITIN, an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number, is for someone who needs a taxpayer number but can't get an SSN. They apply on Form W-7. An ATIN, on Form W-7A, is a temporary number for a child in a pending U.S. adoption. And a PTIN is for paid tax preparers, not taxpayers.
HOOT: And the trap: an ITIN is for tax filing only. It does not authorize work in the United States, and it does not make anyone eligible for Social Security benefits.
ACE: Memory trick: the "I" in ITIN is for "I just need to file." The "A" in ATIN is for "adoption." And the "P" in PTIN is for "preparer," which is me.

ACE: Lulu, stay put, this one is about you. Penny and Milo want to claim a nine-year-old girl who lived with them all of 2025. She's the daughter of Penny's cousin. ACE: Here's why. A qualifying child must pass the relationship test: your child, stepchild, foster child, sibling, half-sibling or step-sibling, or a descendant of any of them, like a grandchild, niece or nephew. A cousin isn't on that list, and neither is a cousin's child. So Lulu fails the relationship test as a qualifying child.
HOOT: Then she goes to the other club. A qualifying relative must not be anyone's qualifying child. Lulu did not live with her mother, Dee, so she is not Dee's qualifying child. She must either live with the taxpayer all year as a member of the household, or be on the relative list. Lulu lived there all year. Her gross income must be under $5,200 for 2025. Four thousand is under. And the taxpayer must provide more than half her support. They did.
ACE: Memory trick for the qualifying child tests: Really Annoying Relatives Steal Juice. Relationship, Age, Residency, Support, Joint return.

ACE: Milo, last one for you. You get a letter from the IRS called a CP2000. What is it? A, a bill you must pay immediately. B, a notice that you're being arrested. C, a proposed change because income reported to the IRS doesn't match your return, which you should agree with or explain by the deadline. D, junk mail.
MILO: I've gotten one of these. I think. Penny opened it. It's C!
[correct]
ACE: Right. A CP2000 is an underreporter notice. Every 1099 and W-2 has a twin copy at the IRS. When the twins don't match the return, the IRS proposes a change. It's not a bill and it's not an audit, but you have to respond by the date on the notice. Agree and follow its instructions, or disagree and explain in writing with documents. Keep a copy.
HOOT: The other notices. A CP14 is a bill: a balance is due. A CP12 says the IRS corrected an error on your return and your refund changed. Letter 5071C asks you to verify your identity before the return is processed.
HOOT: And the scam test. The IRS does not start contact by text, email or social media. It does not demand gift cards or wire transfers. It does not threaten to have you arrested. If someone calls you and does that, hang up.
ACE: Memory trick: CP2000 is the "two thousand questions" letter. It asks; it doesn't bill. CP14 is the bill, fourteen days to feel bad about it.
HOOT: That is not in the regulations.
ACE: It's a memory trick, Hoot. It doesn't have to be in the regulations.
